Symptoms

So, what is astigmatism?

Astigmatism is a condition where, instead of being round, your eyes are slightly egg‑shaped.1 It usually causes blurry vision at all distances. Double vision (often called "ghosting") is also common.2 Many people see glare, halos, streaks, or starburst patterns around lights.

Compensating for astigmatism can cause headaches and eye strain. It can also make driving at night difficult or dangerous.

Most cases of astigmatism can be treated with custom-made glasses or contact lenses. Some types of astigmatism require more specialized treatments like scleral lenses or eye surgery.3 These treatments can cost thousands of dollars in the US and Canada, putting them out of reach for many people.4,5 Even with proper correction, symptoms often don't go away completely. Using a screen can be uncomfortable even if vision is clear otherwise.

How does astigmatism impact people using digital interfaces?

Blur and ghosting can make text much harder to read.6 Sometimes, text may appear to vibrate.

Bright elements on dark backgrounds can cause a glow effect called halation.7 The light color can look like it "bleeds" into the space around it, making text and UI elements look fuzzy or shimmery.

In my own experience, most "dark mode" settings are unusable because of halation. Light themes can still cause me problems, especially if the contrast is very high (like pure black text on a pure white background). I often see faint "copies" of text in between the lines.

The combined effect is that astigmatism increases eye strain and makes websites harder to use.

What should I keep in mind when I design UI?

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines are always a good place to start. Unfortunately, they do not contain any guidance for astigmatism.8 I've come up with a rough list, combining various sources with personal experience.9,10 It's not a comprehensive list, but it should cover the main points:

  • Avoid using pure black text on a pure white background (or vice versa). A small decrease in color contrast will reduce distortion. (Don't reduce the contrast too much, though! Adequate color contrast is part of accessibility for people with low vision.)
  • If your website uses a "dark mode" theme (light text on a dark background), provide a "light mode" alternative if feasible.
  • Use a font that's easy to read. This page uses Lexend, which is my personal favorite. Other good options include Verdana, Calibri, and Atkinson Hyperlegible.
  • Use a large font size. Bigger fonts are easier to read when they get blurry.
  • Use a tall line height and wide letter spacing. More room between lines and letters stops them from overlapping when they blur or duplicate.
  • Be careful with bold and italic text. Both can be harder to read. Use them sparingly.

You might notice that a lot of these design tips also apply for dyslexia! Accessible design for one group often benefits others.
